Mengapa kita masih membiayai petani seolah-olah mereka pekedai?

Bagaimana penanaman berasaskan permintaan, AI dan terma bayaran balik yang mengikut kitaran tanaman boleh membuka sektor pertanian kepada usahawan Malaysia generasi baharu, dan apa yang perlu benar supaya ia berjaya.

Ringkasan pantas
  • Pada 2025, Malaysia masih mengimport sebahagian besar halia (82.7%), cili (67.9%) dan kubis bulat (59.0%). Pasarannya sudah sedia ada, tetapi hanya untuk penanam yang mampu bersaing dari segi kos sampai ke pembeli, kesegaran, konsistensi dan keselamatan makanan.
  • Mulakan dengan pembeli, bukan dengan benih. AI dan alat yang lebih mudah boleh membantu menentukan apa yang hendak ditanam, di mana, bila menuai dan siapa yang akan membeli.
  • Petani berbelanja berbulan-bulan sebelum kutipan pertama yang bermakna. Bayaran balik yang bermula sebelum tuaian boleh mencetuskan kegagalan bayar yang cuba dielakkan. Pemberi pembiayaan wajar menyemak sama ada terma mereka mengikut kitaran tanaman dan kutipan.
  • Teknologi tinggi tidak semestinya ladang menegak. Di negara yang bermandi cahaya matahari sepanjang tahun, fertigasi, penanaman terlindung dan hidroponik cahaya semula jadi sering lebih masuk akal dari segi komersial.
  • Malaysia sudah mempunyai banyak badan sokongan pertanian. Satu laluan masuk yang diselaraskan boleh menjadikannya jauh lebih mudah digunakan oleh pendatang baharu.

Jika seorang jurutera, pembangun perisian atau usahawan muda Malaysia ingin membuka ladang moden esok, dari mana dia harus bermula? Adakah dia tahu apa yang hendak ditanam, di mana mencari tanah, teknologi apa yang perlu dibeli, siapa yang akan membeli hasilnya dan institusi mana yang akan membiayainya?

Dan adakah pihak bank faham bahawa wang pertama yang bermakna mungkin hanya masuk lima atau enam bulan selepas ringgit pertama dibelanjakan?

Tulisan ini memberi tumpuan kepada PMKS pengeluar makanan, bukan syarikat perladangan besar. Soalannya mudah: bagaimanakah rupa teknologi, pembeli dan pembiayaan yang diperlukan supaya perniagaan pertanian kecil boleh berdiri secara komersial?

01 · Mengapa pertanian wajar dilihat semula

Dalam fikiran ramai, bertani masih sinonim dengan kerja kudrat, pendapatan tidak menentu dan nasib cuaca. Risiko itu belum hilang. Yang berubah ialah sejauh mana ia kini boleh diukur dan diurus.

Tanaman boleh dipantau dari jauh. Pengairan boleh diautomasikan. Permintaan boleh diramal, jadual tanaman diselaraskan dan hasil dikesan sepanjang rantaian bekalan.

Maklumat yang dahulunya sukar diperoleh petani dan bank kini boleh dikumpul dan dianalisis. Persoalannya, apakah maknanya bagi perusahaan kecil yang tidak memiliki tanah luas, rangkaian pengedaran atau modal besar?

03 · Pengajaran dari Cameron Highlands

Cameron Highlands antara kisah kejayaan pertanian paling dikenali di negara ini. Kelebihannya melangkaui cuaca sejuk. Beberapa generasi penanam telah membina kepakaran, hubungan pembekal, rangkaian pengedaran dan infrastruktur sokongan.

Pendatang baharu di tempat lain mungkin memiliki teknologi lebih canggih, tetapi masih sukar menandingi hubungan dagangan yang sudah lama terjalin itu.

Adakah ia dikuasai kartel?

Soalan ini wajar dikaji dengan serius, tetapi dakwaan dan bukti adalah dua perkara berbeza. Kartel bermaksud penyelarasan haram antara pesaing. Penumpuan pasaran, hubungan dagangan yang lama dan kuasa membeli yang besar tidak dengan sendirinya membuktikan wujudnya kartel.

Apa kata data

Kajian pasaran sektor makanan MyCC pada 2019 meneliti sawi dan kubis bulat di peringkat nasional, bukan Cameron Highlands secara khusus. Ia mendapati pasaran dikuasai pemborong, dan kuasa tawar-menawar petani umumnya rendah kerana bergantung pada orang tengah serta harga yang kurang telus.

Kajian itu juga menyorot permit import (AP) kubis bulat yang dijual atau disewakan kepada pihak lain, serta amalan "mengikut harga" yang boleh membawa kepada penetapan harga secara bersepakat.

Antara perkara yang wajar disiasat hari ini:

  • Adakah petani mempunyai cukup pembeli alternatif?
  • Adakah segelintir pengedar memegang kuasa harga yang berlebihan?
  • Adakah penanam baharu berdepan halangan yang tidak munasabah untuk memasuki pasaran?
  • Adakah harga di ladang menampung kos pengeluaran?
  • Adakah pemilikan dan hubungan komersial menyekat persaingan?

Kesimpulan yang muktamad memerlukan data transaksi semasa, maklumat pemilikan benefisial dan bahagian pasaran. Kajian 2019 hanyalah titik mula, bukan bukti salah laku pada hari ini.

Benarkah orang tengah punca masalah?

Orang tengah melakukan kerja yang jika tidak, terpaksa dibuat sendiri oleh petani. Mereka mengutip, mengangkut dan menggred hasil, mencari pembeli, kadangkala menanggung rugi stok yang tidak terjual, dan kerap menyediakan modal pusingan. MyCC mendapati ramai petani bergantung pada orang tengah, antara lain untuk membiayai input pertanian.

Kajian akademik memberi sedikit gambaran tempatan. Kajian Sains Malaysiana 2021 oleh Nusrat Munawar, Yang Farina dan rakan-rakan mengesan sisa racun organoklorin, organofosforus dan piretroid dalam sayur dari tujuh kawasan di Cameron Highlands.

Awas

Mengesan racun perosak tidak sama dengan pendedahan yang membahayakan. Risiko bergantung pada jenis bahan, kepekatannya, jumlah yang dimakan dan sifat toksikologinya.

Bolehkah pertanian yang lebih selamat menjadi peluang perniagaan?

Penanam boleh menonjol dengan piawaian keselamatan yang disahkan pihak bebas: pengurusan perosak bersepadu, kawalan biologi, rekod semburan digital, kebolehkesanan dan ujian makmal.

Pembeli boleh mengimbas kod QR untuk melihat di mana dan bila sayur itu ditanam serta pemeriksaan yang telah dilalui. Namun kod QR tidak membuktikan apa-apa tanpa rekod yang boleh dipercayai di belakangnya.

Sama ada pelanggan sanggup membayar cukup untuk menampung kos tambahan masih perlu diuji. Jika sanggup, keselamatan makanan boleh membuka pintu ke pasar raya premium, hospital dan hotel.

12 · Mengapa kita masih membiayai petani seperti pekedai?

Pekedai membeli stok dan mungkin mula menjualnya pada minggu yang sama. Petani membeli benih, baja dan input lain, membayar pekerja, menjaga tanaman dan menunggu ia membesar. Selepas menuai pun, dia mungkin masih perlu menunggu pembeli menjelaskan invois.

Jika bank mahukan bayaran pokok yang besar dalam beberapa bulan pertama, bayaran itu akan menyedut tunai yang diperlukan untuk menghabiskan kitaran tanaman. Struktur bayaran balik itu sendiri boleh mencetuskan masalah kredit yang cuba dielakkan bank.

Ini tidak bermakna bank di Malaysia tidak fleksibel. Pada 21 Julai 2026, Agrobank melancarkan Agro Padi-i Salam, kemudahan berasaskan kontrak Bai' Salam untuk membiayai kos penanaman pesawah. Pakej modal kerja padinya juga membenarkan pesawah membayar balik selepas menuai.

Persoalan sebenar ialah sama ada struktur seumpama ini boleh diperluaskan kepada lebih banyak PMKS pertanian.

The short version
  • In 2025 Malaysia still imported most of its ginger (82.7%), chilli (67.9%) and round cabbage (59.0%). That is a ready market, but only for growers who can win on delivered cost, freshness, consistency and food safety.
  • Start with the buyer, not the seed. AI and simpler tools can help decide what to plant, where, when to harvest and who will buy.
  • A farmer spends for months before the first meaningful collection. Repayment that starts before harvest can cause the very default it is meant to prevent. Lenders should check whether their terms follow crop and collection cycles.
  • High-tech does not have to mean vertical farms. In a country with sun all year, fertigation, protected cultivation and natural-light hydroponics often make more commercial sense.
  • Malaysia already has many agricultural support bodies. A single, coordinated entry pathway could make them far easier for a newcomer to use.

If a young Malaysian engineer, software developer or entrepreneur wanted to start a modern farm tomorrow, where would they begin? Would they know what to grow, where to find land, which technology to buy, who would buy the produce and which lender would finance it?

And would the lender understand that the first meaningful cash may arrive five or six months after the first ringgit is spent?

This piece looks at food-producing MSMEs, not large plantation companies. It asks what technology, buyers and financing would have to look like for smaller agricultural businesses to work commercially.

01 · Why agriculture deserves another look

Farming is still linked in most minds with manual labour, uncertain income and the weather. Those risks have not gone away. What has changed is how well they can be measured and managed.

Crops can now be monitored remotely. Irrigation can be automated. Demand can be forecast, planting schedules coordinated and produce traced through the supply chain.

Information that farmers and lenders once struggled to obtain can now be collected and analysed. The question is what that means for small enterprises without large landholdings, distribution networks or deep pockets.

03 · What Cameron Highlands can teach us

Cameron Highlands is one of Malaysia's best-known farming stories. Its advantage goes well beyond the cool climate. Generations of growers have built expertise, supplier relationships, distribution networks and supporting infrastructure.

A newcomer elsewhere may have better technology and still struggle against those relationships.

Is it controlled by a cartel?

The question deserves a serious look, but allegation and evidence are different things. A cartel means unlawful coordination between competitors. Concentration, long trading relationships and buying power do not by themselves prove it.

What the data says

MyCC's 2019 food sector market review looked at sawi and round cabbage nationally, not Cameron Highlands specifically. It found the market is dominated by wholesalers, and that farmers' bargaining power is generally low because they depend on middlemen and prices are not transparent.

It also flagged import permits (APs) for round cabbage being sold or rented on to other players, and a price-following practice that could lead to concerted pricing.

The concerns worth investigating today:

  • Do farmers have enough alternative buyers?
  • Do a few distributors hold disproportionate pricing power?
  • Do new growers face unreasonable barriers to market access?
  • Do farm-gate prices cover production costs?
  • Do ownership and commercial ties restrict competition?

A firm conclusion would need current transaction data, beneficial ownership and market shares. The 2019 review is a starting point, not proof of present conduct.

Are middlemen really the problem?

Intermediaries do work farmers would otherwise have to do. They collect, transport and grade produce, find buyers, sometimes absorb losses on unsold stock, and often lend working capital. MyCC found many farmers rely on middlemen partly to finance their inputs.

So removing middlemen is not automatically the answer. Competing distribution channels, transparent prices and better bargaining power may do more.

The periods differ and sampling methods may too, so the years are not strictly comparable. I did not obtain the primary monitoring records. The figures do not show that vegetables from Cameron Highlands are unsafe.

Watch

Detecting a pesticide is not the same as harmful exposure. Risk depends on the substance, its concentration, how much is eaten and its toxicology.

Could safer farming become a business?

Growers could stand out with independently verified safety standards: integrated pest management, biological controls, digital spray records, traceability and laboratory testing.

A shopper could scan a QR code and see where and when the vegetables were grown and which checks they passed. A QR code proves nothing, though, without reliable records behind it.

Whether customers will pay enough to cover the extra cost still has to be tested. If they will, food safety could open doors to premium supermarkets, hospitals and hotels.

The demand-led model starts from supermarket specifications, restaurant use, hotel demand and food manufacturers' production schedules. Farmers then organise cultivation around those needs.

Malaysia already has a foundation. Under FAMA's Ladang Kontrak, participants sign forward agreements and FAMA buys their produce, with floor prices as protection when market prices fall. The opportunity is to extend the idea through digital coordination, several buyers and better-fitting finance.

For the experts · How much space?

500 kg a week at 250 g a head and 10% losses means sowing about 2,223 plants each week. Five batches growing at once need about 11,115 positions. At 20 plants per m² that is roughly 556 m² of net growing area, before aisles and downtime. My calculation.

Just-in-time farming still needs slack. Crops do not always mature on schedule, and buyers change their orders. The system needs backup growers, several buyers and production buffers.

12 · Why are we still financing farmers like shopkeepers?

A shopkeeper buys stock and may start selling it the same week. A farmer buys seed, fertiliser and other inputs, pays workers, looks after the crop and waits for it to grow. After harvest, the farmer may still wait for the buyer to pay.

The farmer is RM85,000 out of pocket before the first collection arrives
Cumulative cash position, RM'000. Monthly spending 35, 20, 15, 15, 10, 5; collections 0, 0, 0, 0, 30, 110. My illustrative assumptions, before financing costs.

If a lender wants substantial principal repayments in the first few months, those payments drain the cash needed to finish the crop. The repayment structure can then cause the credit problem the lender was trying to avoid.

This does not mean Malaysian banks are inflexible. On 21 July 2026 Agrobank launched Agro Padi-iSalam, a Bai' Salam facility for paddy farmers' cultivation costs. Its paddy working-capital package also lets farmers repay after harvest.

The real question is whether similar structures can reach a wider range of agricultural MSMEs.

Why might banks hesitate?

There are legitimate reasons: uncertain yields, volatile prices, weather, thin collateral, technical risk and often weak financial records. Many credit officers are trained to read historical accounts, not crop cycles.

The answer is better risk assessment, not weaker lending standards.

For the experts · What would it cost?

RM20 million portfolio at 80% cover is RM16 million guaranteed. At a 5% annual default rate and 80% loss given default, expected loss on the guaranteed share is RM640,000 a year. A 1% fee brings in RM160,000, a gap of RM480,000.

The fee would need to be about 4.0% of the guaranteed amount to cover expected loss alone. At a 1% fee, defaults would have to stay below about 1.25% a year.

My simplified calculations: constant exposure, proportional cover. Excludes expenses, capital, claim timing and correlated defaults, where one flood can hit a whole cluster.

That is why pricing needs proper actuarial work, risk-based fees and lender risk retention, with explicit government support where policy wants a price below cost.

A newcomer may need one agency for crop advice, another for land, another for technology, another for market access and another for finance. Each may fulfil its own mandate. But who is responsible for the whole business becoming commercially sustainable?

Programmes should be judged by disbursements, farm survival, net farmer income, repeat purchases, food safety compliance and financing performance. Announced allocations and training attendance do not show commercial success.

24 · Malaysia needs a high-tech agriculture MSME gateway

Rather than create another agency, Malaysia could build a coordinated service across existing ones. This is my own proposal, not an existing government service.

One entry point could bring together information on suitable crops, training, available land, technology vendors, buyers, grants and financing. AI could help entrepreneurs prepare first-cut financial projections and point them to the right institutions.

An agriculture-specific pathway in existing online financing referral channels could also show where applicants get stuck. It could reveal whether rejections reflect weak credit, missing documents or unsuitable financing structures, and that evidence could shape better agricultural products.

25 · Be careful not to create a digital cartel

Technology can widen market access, but it can also create new concentration. Imagine one platform controlling buyer access, production forecasts, farmer data, transaction records and financing referrals.

Farmers could become as dependent on that platform as they might be on a dominant distributor today. A modern network should have transparent fees, non-exclusive arrangements, open access, secure data sharing and data portability. Farmers must stay free to sell elsewhere.

My take

Malaysia already has agricultural researchers, commercial farmers, development banks, guarantee institutions, technology companies and large consumer markets. The work is to organise them around smaller enterprises that can stand on their own.

Picture a young entrepreneur who, before investing, knows what supermarkets and restaurants need, gets technical advice on suitable crops, builds a realistic production plan and obtains financing that follows expected collections. AI helps monitor the crop and coordinate deliveries. Shared facilities provide packing, cold storage and independent food safety testing.

The lender gets better information. The buyer gets predictable supply. The consumer sees more of where food comes from. The entrepreneur still has to run a viable business, and the lender still has to lend prudently.

Agricultural risk will not disappear. But Malaysia can get better at identifying, pricing, sharing and managing it. Food security cannot rest forever on asking farmers to carry every commercial uncertainty alone. If Malaysia wants more entrepreneurs to grow its food, it must make agriculture commercially understandable, technologically accessible and financially sustainable.
