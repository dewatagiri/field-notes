Demam Emas Pusat Data Malaysia: Apa Berlaku Apabila Amerika Mengawal Cip?

Malaysia memiliki tanah, bangunan dan bekalan elektrik. Namun peraturan yang ditulis di Washington menentukan siapa boleh menggunakan komputer di dalamnya. Inilah pengajaran tiga minggu pada bulan November.

Azman Taufik · 27 November 2026 · 12 minit bacaan
Komen di LinkedIn
Ringkasan pantas
  • Sejak 19 November, lesen Amerika Syarikat diperlukan untuk menghantar cip AI termaju ke Malaysia, Singapura, Thailand, Indonesia dan Brazil, termasuk untuk memindahkannya di dalam negara-negara itu. Pembeli yang beribu pejabat di AS dikecualikan jika pembekal melapor kepada Washington setiap bulan.
  • Pusat data yang membekalkan kuasa pengkomputeran AI melebihi 12 juta TPP (kira-kira 760 cip kelas H100, anggaran saya) kepada syarikat beribu pejabat di China juga memerlukan lesen, dengan anggapan awal bahawa permohonan akan ditolak. Susunan sedia ada perlu dilesenkan sebelum kira-kira 17 Februari 2027.
  • Pada 26 November, FT dan WSJ melaporkan NVIDIA mengarahkan Dell dan Supermicro menangguhkan penghantaran pelayan AI ke empat negara ini. NVIDIA belum mengesahkannya.
  • Malaysia meluluskan pelaburan berkaitan pusat data bernilai RM385.7 bilion dari 2021 hingga separuh pertama 2026. Sebanyak 143 projek yang diluluskan sehingga Jun 2025 dijangka mewujudkan 1,429 pekerjaan.
  • Bank bergerak lebih awal: kira-kira US$7.75 bilion pinjaman pusat data berkaitan pelanggan China di Asia ditangguhkan atau dikaji semula dalam masa seminggu. Saham YTL Power pula jatuh kira-kira 37% sejak 9 Oktober.

Siapakah sebenarnya yang mengawal sebuah pusat data di Malaysia? Di atas kertas, jawapannya mudah. Tanahnya di Johor atau Negeri Sembilan. Bangunannya didirikan kontraktor tempatan. Elektrik dibekalkan TNB dan air oleh Ranhill. Sebahagian besar pembiayaannya datang daripada bank di Malaysia dan Singapura.

November memberi kita jawapan yang lain. Aset paling bernilai di dalam bangunan itu, iaitu cip AI, boleh disekat di sempadan atau dihadkan penggunaannya bagi pelanggan tertentu melalui peraturan yang ditulis di Washington. Dalam beberapa hari sahaja, Malaysia beralih daripada juara serantau kepada negara yang disifatkan mempunyai "risiko lencongan lebih tinggi daripada biasa".

Artikel ini meneliti apa yang berlaku, siapa yang terdedah dan apa yang boleh dilakukan. Saya cuba memisahkan fakta, laporan media dan pandangan saya sendiri. Apabila sesuatu itu hanya dilaporkan, saya nyatakan begitu.

Prinsipnya: tuan tanah belum tentu tuan rumah

Dalam perniagaan, pihak yang memegang input paling terhad biasanya menentukan syarat. Selama dua dekad, kelebihan Malaysia ialah tanah, tenaga dan kelulusan yang pantas. Dalam era AI, input paling terhad ialah cip, dan cip datang bersama peraturan negara pemiliknya. Kita boleh memiliki setiap bata bangunan, tetapi masih tidak menentukan siapa yang dibenarkan duduk di meja.

01Apa yang berlaku dalam tiga minggu

Rentetan peristiwa bergerak daripada skandal kepada peraturan, kemudian senarai hitam dan akhirnya penangguhan penghantaran.

  • 12 Nov: Ketua Pegawai Eksekutif NVIDIA, Jensen Huang, meletak jawatan atas isu peribadi. Beliau dilantik semula pada 23 November selepas lembaga pengarah memaklumkan telah menerima input daripada pegawai kanan kerajaan AS.
  • 13 Nov: MITI menegaskan ia "tidak pernah memberi pelepasan" kepada Megaspeed dari Singapura atau anak syarikatnya di Malaysia, Speedmatrix. Speedmatrix ialah penyewa yang membawa pelayan dan cipnya sendiri ke pusat data tempatan. Kastam sedang menyemak sama ada cip itu dieksport semula.
  • 19 Nov: Peraturan AS berkuat kuasa.
  • 20 Nov: YTL Power menafikan laporan New York Times bahawa Speedmatrix mengendalikan cip NVIDIA bagi pihak Alibaba di kemudahannya di Johor. Ia masih dakwaan, bukan dapatan.
  • 23 Nov: AS memasukkan tujuh syarikat berkaitan Megaspeed ke dalam Entity List, tiga daripadanya disenaraikan di bawah Malaysia. Keesokan harinya, MITI menasihati pusat data supaya tidak menempatkan atau melayani syarikat itu.
  • 26 Nov: Penangguhan penghantaran oleh NVIDIA dilaporkan oleh FT dan WSJ.

Adakah Megaspeed puncanya? Ia mempercepatkan keadaan. Draf peraturan yang menamakan Malaysia dan Thailand telah dilaporkan sejak Julai 2025, dan firma guaman menyifatkan versi akhirnya sebagai "lama dinanti". Institute for Progress menganggarkan pusat data jarak jauh di rantau ini membekalkan kira-kira suku daripada kuasa pengkomputeran AI China. Menyewakan kuasa pengkomputeran kepada syarikat China ialah amalan biasa di seluruh rantau ini.

04Siapa memiliki ledakan ini, dan siapa menanggung risikonya

Kebanyakan platform besar dimiliki pihak asing. AirTrunk dimiliki konsortium Blackstone dan CPP Investments. Bridge Data Centres dimiliki Bain Capital. Princeton Digital Group disokong Warburg Pincus, Mubadala dan Ontario Teachers'. GDS memegang 19.4% DayOne. Pemilik dan pengendali utama yang tersenarai di Bursa ialah YTL Power dan Telekom Malaysia.

Kebergantungan kepada pelanggan China tidak didedahkan secara rasmi, tetapi petunjuknya jelas. DayOne memperoleh 87% hasil separuh pertama 2026 dari Malaysia, dan 69% hasilnya datang daripada seorang pelanggan yang tidak dinamakan. ByteDance dilaporkan secara meluas sebagai penyewa utamanya. Bridge melayani ByteDance, manakala Digital Halo melayani Alibaba dilaporkan.

Apa kata angka

Sebanyak 143 projek pusat data bernilai RM144.4 bilion yang diluluskan antara 2021 dan Jun 2025 dijangka mewujudkan 1,429 pekerjaan. Itu bersamaan kira-kira RM101 juta pelaburan bagi setiap pekerjaan (kiraan saya). Projek Pasukan Petugas Pusat Data pula dikenakan syarat kandungan tempatan minimum 30%.

Dengan kata lain, Malaysia hari ini kebanyakannya memperoleh pendapatan sebagai tuan tanah, pembina dan penjual elektrik. Pemilikan kuasa pengkomputeran, data dan harta intelek kebanyakannya berada di tangan orang lain.

06Bolehkah Malaysia menggantikan NVIDIA?

Tidak dengan cara yang melepaskan kita daripada peraturan ini. NVIDIA menguasai kira-kira 81% hasil cip AI pusat data pada 2026 menurut anggaran IDC dilaporkan. Namun ujian peraturan AS berasaskan prestasi cip dan teknologi AS, bukan jenama.

  • AMD dan Intel: direka di AS dan tertakluk kepada ujian yang sama.
  • Google TPU, AWS Trainium: kebanyakannya ditawarkan sebagai perkhidmatan awan syarikat AS.
  • Huawei Ascend: panduan BIS pada Mei 2025 menyatakan penggunaannya "di mana-mana di dunia" berisiko melanggar kawalan AS.
  • Membeli melalui negara ketiga: itu lencongan, iaitu perkara yang hendak disekat oleh peraturan ini.
Perangkap

Mempelbagaikan jenama cip tidak mengurangkan pendedahan kepada peraturan AS. Yang lebih penting ialah mempelbagaikan pelanggan.

09Ke mana pelaburan seterusnya boleh pergi

Vietnam, Filipina, India, Jepun dan Australia tidak disenaraikan dalam keperluan lesen lima negara. Namun kawalan akses jarak jauh tetap terpakai di seluruh dunia. Bagi pelanggan yang mahukan laluan paling ringkas, negara yang tidak disenaraikan kini mempunyai satu kelebihan kecil berbanding Johor.

Di dalam negara, Sarawak menarik kerana tenaga hidronya. SoftBank, Grab dan PETROS menandatangani perjanjian pembangunan bersama pada 9 November dengan modal permulaan US$1.85 bilion, dan cita-cita jangka panjang 5 GW atau lebih. Namun Sarawak hanya mempunyai sekitar 17 MW yang beroperasi hari ini, tertakluk kepada peraturan lesen yang sama dengan Johor, dan sambungan antarabangsanya belum disahkan. Beban kerja latihan AI lebih sesuai di sana kerana kurang sensitif kepada jarak.

Siapa untung daripada keadaan sedia ada?

  • Pemilik platform asing: penilaian dibina atas pertumbuhan pantas dengan apa jua penyewa.
  • Penyedia awan GPU: menyewakan kuasa pengkomputeran kepada sesiapa yang membayar.
  • Pemilik tanah dan kontraktor: dibayar ketika membina, bukan ketika bangunan digunakan.
  • Kerajaan: tajuk berita kelulusan mengira ringgit pelaburan, bukan pekerjaan atau pemilikan kuasa pengkomputeran.

Tingkah laku akan berubah jika kelulusan dinilai berdasarkan pekerjaan, pemilikan tempatan dan pematuhan; jika tempahan elektrik dikenakan harga; dan jika pendedahan maklumat diwajibkan oleh undang-undang.

Pandangan saya

Bagi sebahagian besar kapasiti, penilaian saya ialah penyesuaian struktur yang boleh diurus, dan bukannya ancaman asas. Pusat data untuk penyedia awan gergasi AS dijangka mengalami kelewatan, kemudian beroperasi seperti biasa.

Namun November menunjukkan ilusi kedaulatan teknologi kita. Kita memiliki tanah, bangunan, elektrik, air dan sebahagian pembiayaan. Bahagian paling strategik, iaitu kuasa pengkomputeran, dikawal teknologi dan undang-undang negara lain. Menutup pintu kepada pelaburan asing tidak akan menyelesaikan apa-apa. Sebaliknya, jadikan pematuhan sebagai kelebihan, ukur nilai melalui pekerjaan dan pemilikan, dan bina lebih banyak bahagian rantaian nilai di tangan rakyat Malaysia.

Malaysia's Data Centre Gold Rush: What Happens When America Controls the Chips?

Malaysia owns the land, the buildings and the power. A rule written in Washington decides who may use the computers inside. What three weeks in November tell us about the boom.

Azman Taufik · 27 November 2026 · 12 min read
Comment on LinkedIn
The short version
  • Since 19 November, a US licence is needed to ship advanced AI chips to, or move them within, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. Buyers headquartered in the US are excluded if their suppliers report to Washington every month.
  • Data centres that give more than 12 million TPP of AI compute (roughly 760 H100-class chips, my estimate) to firms headquartered in China also need a licence, and face a presumption of denial. Existing setups must be licensed by about 17 February 2027.
  • On 26 November the FT and WSJ reported that NVIDIA told Dell and Supermicro to pause AI server shipments to four of these countries. NVIDIA has not confirmed it.
  • Malaysia approved RM385.7 billion of data-centre-related investment from 2021 to mid-2026. The 143 projects approved up to June 2025 were expected to create 1,429 jobs.
  • Lenders moved first. About US$7.75 billion of China-linked data centre loans in Asia were delayed or put under review in one week. YTL Power is down about 37% since 9 October.

Who actually controls a Malaysian data centre? On paper the answer is easy. The land is in Johor or Negeri Sembilan. A Malaysian contractor put up the building. TNB supplies the power and Ranhill the water. Banks in Malaysia and Singapore financed much of it.

November gave us a different answer. The most valuable thing inside the building, the AI chips, can be stopped at the border or restricted for some customers by a rule written in Washington. Within days, Malaysia went from regional winner to a country with a "higher-than-normal risk of diversion".

This piece covers what happened, who is exposed and what can be done. I have tried to keep facts, media reports and my own views apart. Where something is only reported, I say so.

The principle: owning the building does not mean owning the power

In business, whoever holds the scarcest input usually sets the terms. For twenty years Malaysia's edge was land, power and fast approvals. In AI the scarcest input is the chip, and the chip comes with its home country's rules attached. We can own every brick and still not decide who gets a seat at the table.

01What happened in three weeks

The sequence ran from scandal to rule to blacklist to a shipment pause.

  • 12 Nov: NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang resigned over personal conduct. He was reinstated on 23 November after the board said it had input from senior US officials.
  • 13 Nov: MITI said it had "never cleared" Singapore's Megaspeed or its Malaysian unit Speedmatrix, a tenant that brings its own servers and chips into local data centres. Customs is checking whether any chips were re-exported.
  • 19 Nov: the US rule took effect.
  • 20 Nov: YTL Power denied a New York Times report that Speedmatrix ran NVIDIA chips for Alibaba at its Johor site. That claim is an allegation, not a finding.
  • 23 Nov: the US put seven Megaspeed-linked firms on its Entity List, three listed under Malaysia. The next day, MITI told data centres not to host or serve them.
  • 26 Nov: the NVIDIA pause was reported.

Was Megaspeed the cause? It sped things up. A draft rule naming Malaysia and Thailand was reported in July 2025, and law firms call the final version "long-awaited". The Institute for Progress estimates that remote data centres in this region supply about a quarter of China's AI compute. Renting compute to Chinese firms was common practice across the region.

Two points get little attention. First, a change of use counts as an in-country transfer, so the rule reaches chips already installed. Second, the rule tightens foundry and packaging rules for advanced chips designed by firms in the five countries. That also touches Malaysia's chip-design plans.

"Under construction" can mean about 1.0 GW or 6.0 GW, depending on whether you count the phase being built or the whole campus. Data centres have contracted 5.65 GW from TNB but draw about 1.26 GW. In my view the electricity forecast is the one most at risk.

04Who owns the boom, and who carries the risk

Most of the big platforms are foreign-owned. A Blackstone and CPP Investments consortium owns AirTrunk. Bain Capital owns Bridge Data Centres. Warburg Pincus, Mubadala and Ontario Teachers' back Princeton Digital Group. GDS holds 19.4% of DayOne. The main Bursa-listed owner-operators are YTL Power and Telekom Malaysia.

China-linked exposure is not officially disclosed, but the signs are clear. DayOne earned 87% of its first-half 2026 revenue in Malaysia, and 69% of its revenue came from one customer it does not name. ByteDance is widely reported to be that tenant. Bridge serves ByteDance and Digital Halo serves Alibaba reported.

What the data says

The 143 data centre projects worth RM144.4 billion approved between 2021 and June 2025 were expected to create 1,429 jobs. That is about RM101 million of investment per job (my calculation). Data Centre Task Force projects must meet a minimum of 30% local content.

So today Malaysia earns mostly as landlord, builder and power seller. The compute, the data and the intellectual property mostly belong to others.

06Can Malaysia replace NVIDIA?

Not in a way that escapes this rule. IDC estimates that NVIDIA took about 81% of AI data centre chip revenue in 2026 reported. But the US test is based on chip performance and US technology, not on the brand.

  • AMD and Intel: US-designed and caught by the same test.
  • Google TPU, AWS Trainium: offered mainly as cloud services by US firms.
  • Huawei Ascend: BIS guidance in May 2025 said using these chips "anywhere in the world" risks breaching US controls.
  • Buying through a third country: that is diversion, which is exactly what the rule targets.
Trap

Switching chip brands does not reduce exposure to US rules. Diversifying customers matters more.

Power contracts with TNB are take-or-pay, and the Minister has said operators must pay for all the capacity they reserve. An empty hall still gets a power bill. Most of the large-loan risk sits with international and Singapore banks. Malaysian banks' share is not disclosed.

09Where the next investment could go

Vietnam, the Philippines, India, Japan and Australia are not in the five-country licence requirement. The remote-access control still applies worldwide. For customers who want the simplest route, unlisted countries now have a small edge over Johor.

At home, Sarawak appeals because of its hydropower. SoftBank, Grab and PETROS signed joint development agreements on 9 November with US$1.85 billion of seed capital and a long-term ambition of 5 GW or more. But Sarawak has only about 17 MW running today. It falls under the same licence rule as Johor, and its international connectivity is unverified. AI training workloads suit it best because they are less sensitive to distance.

Who gains from the way things are?

  • Foreign platform owners: valuations built on fast growth with any tenant.
  • GPU cloud providers: renting compute to whoever pays.
  • Landowners and contractors: paid when they build, not when the building is used.
  • Government: approval headlines count ringgit invested, not jobs or compute owned.

Behaviour would change if approvals were scored on jobs, local ownership and compliance, if power reservations carried a price, and if disclosure were required by law.

Questions that need answers

For MITI

How many megawatts in Malaysia are leased to China-headquartered tenants today? There is no public figure yet.

For MIDA

Should every approval report permanent jobs and local ownership next to the ringgit value?

For TNB and the Energy Commission

If 5.65 GW is reserved and 1.26 GW is used, who pays for the grid built for the rest?

For banks

How many of your loans depend on a tenant that now needs a US licence?

For operators

Do you know your customers' customers, all the way up to the ultimate parent?

For investors

Is the price you pay based on contracted megawatts or announced megawatts?

For all of us

Is Malaysia building an AI nation, or becoming a landlord to other people's AI?

A practical plan

1

Bring remote compute under Malaysian law

Require know-your-customer checks up to the ultimate parent for buyers of AI compute. This is the gap the US rule now fills from outside.

Measure: a bill tabled by mid-2027.
2

Register and audit AI chips

A serial-number register reconciled with Customs import records, plus independent facility audits.

Measure: share of installed accelerators registered.
3

Negotiate a named trusted-partner arrangement

The rule itself gives the UAE a presumption of approval with a list of approved entities. That is the clearest precedent.

Measure: number of Malaysian operators on an approved list.
4

Score approvals on value captured

Publish permanent jobs, local equity and local suppliers next to the investment figure.

Measure: permanent jobs per RM1 billion approved, published yearly.
5

Finance MSME suppliers against contracts, not campuses

Invoice and progress-claim financing from tier-1 contractors, with end-client checks and concentration limits per campus.

Measure: MSME supplier financing with end-client look-through.
A call to government
  • Before end-December 2026: publish the megawatts leased to China-headquartered tenants, and notify all 117 approved projects.
  • Before 17 February 2027: set up a joint desk with BIS to help existing operators meet the licence deadline.
  • By mid-2027: table a law on data centre disclosure and remote compute.
  • In the 2027 tariff review: price reserved capacity so that households and MSMEs do not pay for unused grid.

My take

For most of the capacity, my assessment is a manageable structural adjustment rather than a fundamental threat. Data centres built for US hyperscalers should see delays and then carry on.

But November exposed the illusion of our technological sovereignty. We own the land, the buildings, the power, the water and some of the financing. The most strategic part, the computing power, is controlled by other countries' technology and laws. Closing the door to foreign investment would solve nothing. Instead, make compliance a selling point, measure value in jobs and ownership, and build more of the value chain in Malaysian hands.

If you had to choose, would you rather Malaysia host more AI capacity for anyone who pays, or less capacity that we own and control more of ourselves?
