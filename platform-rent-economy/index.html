Are digital platforms becoming the private landlords of the economy?

Fees, rent and dependence in Amazon, DoorDash, Grab, Booking.com and the Malaysian marketplaces. And what happens when AI agents start choosing the seller for us, 2026 to 2076.

The short version
  • The question: can a platform charge far more than its services are worth, because sellers have nowhere else to go? A high fee alone does not prove it.
  • Read the number first. Amazon's US$240.8B from seller services and advertising is not a 50% take rate. DoorDash keeps 13.6% of order value as revenue, but only 1.4% as net profit.

  • The next toll may sit above the marketplace. If AI agents choose and pay for us, power moves to whoever controls recommendation, identity and payment approval. That is my central 2036 view. It is a judgement, not a forecast.

Is a platform fee a price, or a rent? The answer decides whether we need a cap, a rule on access, or nothing at all.

A restaurant pays DoorDash. A homestay pays an online travel agent. A home baker pays Shopee, then pays again for the voucher, the delivery programme and the advertisement that lifts her listing. Each of them says the total is too high. Each of them also says they cannot leave.

This paper tests that complaint. It does not assume the fee is unfair, and it does not assume it is fair. It builds on my earlier draft, corrects several claims in it, and adds new evidence from filings and regulators up to 18 November 2026.

01The question, and what would count as an answer

Main point: a fee is rent only when it exceeds what a competitive market would pay for the service, and the seller cannot walk away.

Platforms do real work. They find buyers, hold payments, check fraud, run delivery and take on risk. A merchant who would otherwise pay for the same work elsewhere is not being exploited by a fee that covers it. That is the honest starting point, and Marketplace Pulse, the research firm behind the most-cited Amazon fee estimates, makes the same point: the services would not be free elsewhere, because Google and Facebook ads and outside warehouses cost money too.

The concern starts when a platform controls the rules of access as well as the market. It can rank rivals, sell the advertising needed to be seen, set the terms for delivery and payment, and hold the data. At that point the fee is no longer only a price for service. It is also the price of staying visible.

For the experts

Working definition of platform rent (my operational use): revenue attributable to control of a scarce access bottleneck, above the competitive return needed to supply the underlying service.

Decomposition: merchant payment = efficient service cost + normal competitive return + bottleneck rent + measurement error. Each term is hard to estimate because platforms share infrastructure and cross-subsidise sides. The rent term is identified only with a counterfactual: a parity-rule change, a payment-rail change, a ranking change or new entry.

I use five tests. A platform is more likely to be earning rent when most of them are true.

  1. Dependence: the merchant cannot replace the platform's sales quickly.
  2. Layering: the platform also controls discovery, delivery, payment or credit, so leaving one layer costs the others.
  3. Opacity: the merchant cannot see how ranking or fees are set.
  4. Fee drift: total charges rise faster than the cost of the services they cover.
  5. Weak entry: rivals come and fail, or never come.

None of the five is proof alone. Together they form a pattern that regulators, courts and researchers can check.

02Read the number before you argue about it

Main point: four different numbers all get called "the take rate". They answer four different questions.

  • Headline commission: the percentage on the fee schedule.
  • Seller expense ratio: everything the seller pays the platform, as a share of sales. This includes fulfilment, advertising, storage and returns.
  • Platform net revenue margin: the platform's recognised revenue divided by order value.
  • Platform profit margin: what is left after the platform's own costs.

Trap: adding two Amazon lines

Amazon reported US$172.2B of third-party seller services and US$68.6B of advertising for 2025. Added together that is US$240.8B, or about 34% of net sales (my calculation). It looks like proof of a huge cut from sellers. It is not.

The advertising line also covers vendors, publishers, authors and brands that are not marketplace sellers. Dividing by sellers' total sales gives a number nobody can defend. The right use of the US$240.8B is scale: it shows how large the monetisation of the marketplace has become.
