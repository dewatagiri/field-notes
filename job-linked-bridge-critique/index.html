The Poverty Trap Is Not a Failure of the System. It Is the System Working.

I built a loan engine for tradespeople, then spent a week trying to argue against it. This is what I found.

By Azman Taufik · Companion to how the engine thinks · Code: github.com/dewatagiri/self_employed_bridge

I should say up front that I don't think anyone designed it this way. Nobody sat in a room and decided to trap painters in debt. The incentives just end up there anyway, and my own engine is a good example. It pays the painter's paint supplier directly, collects from the customer through a controlled account, and splits any loss between a development institution, a guarantor and a lender. On paper it works fine. What I should have asked sooner is who it works for.

1 · Where this comes fromWe're copying a dream that already failed

In the US, wages stopped keeping up and the fix wasn't giving workers more bargaining power. It was giving them more credit. Credit cards, subprime loans, buy-now-pay-later, all sold as inclusion. A lot of people ended up as contractors doing the physical work, carrying the liability, and owning very little at the end of the week.

Much of the developing world is now copying that. "Financial inclusion" too often means plugging the people at the bottom into the same pipes, just with better APIs.

A bridge loan for a tradesperson could be the opposite of that. It could also be the same thing in a smaller package. The design decides which, and that's what this piece is about.

2 · The controlThe controlled account is what makes the loan work, and what it costs the painter

My engine makes the customer pay into an account the lender controls, so the loan is repaid before the painter touches the money. That isn't a minor feature. It's the number that decides whether the product can exist at all. In my base case, if just 5% of customers pay the painter in cash instead, the development institution and the guarantor together lose about RM210 on a typical RM10,000 loan. Get that below roughly 1% and it starts to work.

Controlling the account matters more than the price.

Now look at it from the painter's side. He can't haggle for a cash discount. He can't hold cash between jobs. He can't manage his own cash flow. The supplier and the lender get paid first, and he gets whatever's left. The engine is upfront about this, but being upfront isn't the same as the painter agreeing to it. A regulator or a funder should see this trade-off before the first loan goes out.

3 · The loss waterfallCharity takes the first loss, the lender takes the profit

The way losses are shared is simple. The first-loss reserve takes the first RM50,000 of every group of 100 loans. The guarantor takes 70% of what's left. The lender takes the rest. Here's how that plays out in my base case. These are my assumptions, not findings, because there's no Malaysian loss data to use:

90%of expected loss lands on the reserve
7%on the guarantor
3%on the lender
37%of groups use up the whole reserve

That's a development tool doing what it was built to do, which is to carry risk a commercial lender won't touch. But if you look at it plainly, it's also cheap insurance for the lender. If the reserve is public or philanthropic money, you have to ask what the borrower actually gets for that subsidy. A lender who earns a margin on loans where someone else carries nearly all the risk hasn't learned to serve this customer. He's just learned to get paid for standing nearby.

The buyer isn't in the picture at all. The engine counts any deposit the customer has already paid and only lends the gap, but it doesn't ask for one. A homeowner who pays nothing up front still gets his paint, and the painter ends up borrowing against his own future labour to cover the buyer's cash flow.

4 · The treadmillA ladder, or a toll booth?

Picture a painter who takes a loan on every job to buy RM3,000 of paint. He isn't climbing anything. He's paying a toll to the supplier, the payment rail and the lender every time he wants permission to work. The engine tracks defaults, recoveries, how fast the reserve runs down, and the lender's profit. It also checks whether borrowers are new to formal credit. That tells you who got access. It doesn't tell you whether anyone got ahead.

Nothing in the engine asks whether the painter finishes the year with more of his own money than he started with.

5 · Getting outOne bad week shouldn't count forever

The engine treats paying late and paying around the lender as two different things, on purpose. A late payment is just late. If someone pays the painter in cash, they can cure it by repaying in full and getting an officer's approval. A second time blocks them for the rest of the pilot. I think that's a reasonable rule, but it was written by people who haven't had a child end up in the emergency room, or a client sit on an invoice for two months. Before this blocks anyone for good, a pilot should find out how often an honest delay looks like a bypass.

Three things I'd change next
  • A way out. Put 5% to 10% of every settled job into a savings pot the worker owns. Once it covers three jobs' worth of materials, the engine stops lending to him, because he now has his own working capital.
  • Put the buyer's money first. Ask for a deposit before the worker is assessed, and charge late-paying customers a penalty that goes to the worker.
  • Room for emergencies. Give reliable workers one 60-day pause that doesn't count against them.
None of these is in the engine today. They're ideas.

6 · What to measureThe number that's missing

If an engine only reports lender profit and default rates, it only proves that the cage is well built. The number I'd add is the graduation rate: out of all the small tradespeople who borrow, how many build up enough capital to tell the lender they don't need them any more.

If we don't measure graduation, we aren't breaking the cycle. We're automating it.

One thing I'll give the engine. Its rules are in open code, not hidden in a vendor contract, and the assumptions sit in a file anyone can change. So the weak spots I've described are easy for anyone to see. That's why I think it's worth fixing and not just worth criticising.
