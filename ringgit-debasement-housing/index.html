Affiliate Marketing in Malaysia

How far it can grow, how it will shape culture, and how to benefit: 2023–2029

Objective

This brief answers three questions about affiliate marketing in Malaysia:

  • How much more can it grow?
  • How will it shape culture?
  • How can an individual or business benefit?

It then tests whether the current direction is the right one, and whether the government is supportive or conservative on regulation. It combines three earlier research reports with new research on market size, Budget 2027, the Online Safety Act, tax guidance and consumer-protection plans (all dated and sourced at the end).

1. The short answers

2. Background: how we got here

2.1 From search to social in three phases

Source: Ipsos Malaysia 2025 and 2026 reports. Platform figures are multi-platform participation, not GMV share. A two-point dip is not proof of contraction.

TikTok Shop combines discovery, demonstration, questions and payment in one feed. The affiliate’s job therefore changed: instead of sending traffic to a shop, the creator builds desire, explains the product and closes the sale. Shoppers also changed what they value. From 2023 to 2025 the top platform-selection factors moved from quality and promises kept towards promotions, variety, value for money, ease of use and fast delivery.

2.3 The reach ceiling

About 98% of Malaysians are online and about 85% are active on social media, spending over 7.5 hours a day online across roughly eight platforms a month. Audience growth is no longer the lever. Competition is for attention and conversion:

  • Vertical 9:16 video of 15–60 seconds is the baseline asset. Phone-shot, creator-style content beats studio-grade ads.
  • Ad fatigue set in as audiences met identical scripts. Macro-influencer rates rose while engagement fell, so budgets moved to micro and nano creators (about 1,000–50,000 followers).
  • Social platforms took about half of tracked digital ad spend in Q2 2025.

2.5 Who buys, and what

TikTok Shop is no longer a youth story. Ipsos identifies ages 25–44 as the growth engine: TikTok Shop use rose from 52% to 63% among 25–34s and from 40% to 59% among 35–44s between 2025 and 2026. For categories, the share of recent online buyers who bought each type in 2026 was: fashion 63%, home care 57%, groceries 47%, beauty 45%, accessories 37%, personal care 28%, healthcare products 25% and electronic devices 19%. These swing year to year, so treat them as direction, not trend lines.

On ethnicity, no credible public dataset links affiliate purchases to ethnic group. What the evidence supports is different routes to credibility: Ipsos finds TikTok Shop especially popular among Malays and in East Coast and northern regions, and Xiaohongshu skews Chinese Malaysian and female. Buying tendencies beyond that (for example halal sensitivity, or research-heavy shopping) are hypotheses to test, not measured facts. Segment by language, life stage, household role and platform behaviour instead.

2.6 How affiliate earns, and its limits

Old modelNew model
ChainBrand → agency → influencer → audienceBrand → affiliate creator → sale → commission
PaymentFixed fee up frontCommission when the order completes, often with a fee
VisibilityLow attributionPlatform-tracked sales and payouts

A 2024 regional study of 371 TikTok Shop affiliate listings found 6%–12% the most common commission range (averages near 10% fashion, 9% beauty, 8% electronics). This is regional and historical, not a current Malaysian rate card.

Worked example: a RM60 product at 8% pays RM4.80 gross per eligible sale. RM3,000 gross commission needs 625 eligible sales, before production cost, paid promotion, tax and reversed commissions. Judge any affiliate activity by net income per hour and by how steady it is.

3. Question 1: how much more can affiliate grow?

3.1 Where the market stands

  • Malaysia is among the fastest-growing platform e-commerce markets in Southeast Asia. Momentum Works reports platform GMV growth of 47.6% in 2025 (19.5% in 2024), second only to Thailand at 51.8%. The region grew 22.8% to US$157.6B.
  • Content-led commerce is now a third of the region’s platform sales. Momentum Works puts content commerce at US$49.7B, 32% of platform GMV in 2025, up from 20% in 2024, and projects US$77.9B in 2026.
  • TikTok Shop Malaysia is about US$5B in GMV (2025). This figure comes from secondary compilations citing Momentum Works; treat it as approximate. Live selling is reported at roughly a third of TikTok Shop sales regionally and in Malaysia (TikTok Shop Summit, 20 August 2026, as reported by an industry guide).
  • Hypergrowth is already slowing. TikTok Shop’s regional growth fell from about 118% in 2024 to 54% in 2025, against Shopee’s 24.5% (Market Maze analysis of Momentum Works data). Momentum Works itself asks whether live commerce growth is truly incremental, or just taking sales from other channels.

3.2 Why published forecasts disagree

Published forecasts for “Malaysia social commerce” cannot be compared. A 2024 market report forecast the whole segment at US$4.5B by 2029, yet TikTok Shop Malaysia alone is already near US$5B by GMV in 2025. A 2025 report forecasts 16.1% annual growth for 2025–2030. The gap comes from definitions: what is counted, GMV versus revenue, and which platforms. No source we found measures affiliate-attributed sales in Malaysia. So there is no hard number for “affiliate market size”, and anyone quoting one is estimating.

3.3 Our scenarios

Because affiliate sales sit inside content commerce, we use TikTok Shop Malaysia GMV as a proxy for the pool affiliates sell into. These are illustrations built on simple growth assumptions, not forecasts from a published source.

Scenario20262027202820292029 vs 2025

Assumptions: 2025 base of about US$5.0B; growth decelerates each year because the base grows, buyers have plateaued (Ipsos) and regional TikTok Shop growth is already falling. The high case assumes Malaysia keeps growing near its 2025 pace for another year.

What this means for affiliates: each 10% of that GMV routed through affiliates at an 8% average commission is worth 0.8% of GMV. In 2029 that is roughly US$62M (low), US$82M (base) or US$103M (high) in commissions per 10 percentage points of affiliate share. The share itself is unknown. The pool grows, but the number of people competing for it is growing too, so income per affiliate will not rise as fast as the market.

Reading: affiliate commerce has years of growth left, but the easy phase is over. The likely path is a shift from explosive growth to steady double-digit growth, with a bigger share going to operators who can show trust, proof and compliance.

4. Question 2: how will affiliate shape culture?

Evidence on culture is thinner than on market size. Most hard data comes from platform-sponsored research or surveys. The shifts below are supported by evidence where noted; the overall reading is our judgement.

ShiftWhat the evidence showsWhat it means
1. Trust moves from brands to peersKearney’s study for TikTok found about 80% of regular Malaysian users use the platform for learning, cultural connection and stress relief (sponsored). Nano and micro creators dominate Xiaohongshu’s Malaysian creator base (46.9% and 44.9%, per one industry guide)Recommendation becomes the unit of commerce. A small creator with proof beats a celebrity with reach
2. Shopping becomes entertainmentLive selling uses countdown timers, vouchers and free gifts; commentators call it “the new pasar malam”. A third of TikTok Shop sales are reported to come from liveBuying is social and emotional, not just rational. Hosts need warmth and credibility
3. A doorway to livelihoodTikTok Shop reports 1.8M local businesses; 97% of surveyed businesses say TikTok matters to revenue and 50% say it drives over 40% of sales (sponsored). Over 100,000 MSMEs trained, many semi-urban or ruralLower barrier to entry for micro-entrepreneurs. Malaysia-specific data on how concentrated income is was not found
4. Impulse buying becomes normalA Consumer Forum of Malaysia study reported over 60% of social media users make unplanned purchases; in the same reporting, nearly half spent over RM200 and one in three regretted a buy (iMoney, 2025). A 2026 academic study links TikTok marketing to Gen Z impulse buyingDebt, clutter and regret are real costs. Expect more pressure for responsible-selling norms
5. Informal becomes formalLHDN guidance (14 January 2026) covers anyone earning from online influence, including students and homemakers; affiliate commissions and free products count. Complaints about misleading ads reached 2,608 in 2023Side income becomes taxed business income. Disclosure and honest claims become social expectations
6. Feeds become regulated public spaceUnder-16s cannot register accounts from 1 June 2026; all users face age verification against government IDSociety now treats social feeds like places that need rules. Brands and creators must design for adults and for verified identity
Net effect (our judgement): affiliate commerce professionalises peer selling. Credibility becomes the scarce asset. The regulators are pushing the same way, with disclosure, identity checks and tax, so the culture is likely to drift from casual hype towards accountable recommendation. The cost is more impulse spending and the risk of reputation damage for creators who oversell.

5. Is the government supportive or conservative?

The honest answer is both. It depends on the area. The table sorts what the government has done into supportive, protective and open.

Verdict

The government is pro-adoption and pro-accountability. It wants Malaysians selling online, small businesses digitised and rural products reaching platforms. It is strict on child safety, identity, tax and platform and foreign-seller accountability. It has not tried to restrict affiliate marketing itself, and it has not yet written influencer-specific rules.

For an honest, local, tax-compliant operator, this mix mostly helps: it cuts scam competition and favours local MSME products. For operators who rely on exaggerated claims, undisclosed promotion, foreign drop-shipping or unreported income, the direction is clearly against them.

What to watch next

  • E-Commerce Bill text: how it treats platforms, sellers and, if at all, affiliates and live hosts.
  • Any disclosure rule for paid and commission-based content. None exists yet in specific form.
  • Impact of verified-advertiser rules on affiliates who run paid promotion.
  • LHDN enforcement of the influencer guidelines, and any online dispute-resolution platform from KPDN.

6.2 Rules of play

  • Disclose every commercial relationship clearly, and avoid claims you cannot prove, especially in health and beauty.
  • Treat income as taxable from day one: register, keep seven years of records and track free products at fair value.
  • Prefer verified, local sellers and trade within the platform; the KPDN minister noted that most reported e-commerce scams happen off-platform.
  • Own part of the audience through WhatsApp, Telegram or a mailing list, so one algorithm change does not end the business.
  • Make the claim believable: test the product, show the proof, say who should not buy it.
  • Do not target minors. Under-16s are no longer meant to hold accounts.

7.2 Are we on the right track?

Yes, with conditions. The direction of the market and the direction of policy both reward the same behaviour. The track is right if it follows the left column below and wrong if it follows the right.

On track ifOff track if
Built on real expertise, tested products and honest claimsBuilt on follower counts and viral hype
Local MSME and verified-seller productsUnverified foreign drop-shipping, which the new E-Commerce Bill targets
Full disclosure of paid and commission relationshipsUndisclosed promotion or inflated “original price” tricks
Income recorded and tax-ready (LHDN guidance)Untracked, unreported commissions and gifts
Several platforms plus an owned audience (WhatsApp, community)All sales through one platform and one algorithm
Adult audiences and verified advertising accountsContent aimed at minors or unverified paid promotion

7.3 What would change this view

  • An E-Commerce Bill that places direct liability or licensing duties on affiliates and live hosts.
  • A sharp fall in online-buyer participation in Ipsos’s next survey, or a clear sign that live sales are cannibalising other channels rather than adding new demand.
  • Material cuts to platform commission rates or affiliate programme terms.
  • New consumer-credit or impulse-selling rules aimed at live commerce.
Bottom line: affiliate marketing in Malaysia will keep growing, more slowly, and will reward trust over reach. The government is not against it: it is against harm, opacity, unfair foreign competition and untaxed income. Operators who are expert, local, transparent and compliant are on the right track.
